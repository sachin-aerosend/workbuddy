How to Stop Doomscrolling: 8 Small Changes That Actually Work

By Sachin Sahani · 19 September 2026 · 3 min read

Doomscrolling isn't a character flaw, it's a design problem. These eight small, realistic changes help you scroll less without quitting social media entirely.

"Doomscrolling" started as a word for compulsively reading bad news, but most of us now use it for any endless scroll we can't seem to stop: news, Reels, Shorts, TikTok, X.

Here's the reassuring part: you don't need a digital detox retreat or a flip phone. Small changes to when and how you scroll make a big difference.

Why it's so hard to stop

Feeds are built to remove every natural stopping point. There's no last page, the next item loads before you decide, and every so often something is genuinely interesting, which keeps you hoping the next one will be too. Add tiredness or stress and the "just one more" loop runs itself.

So the goal isn't to try harder. It's to put stopping points back.

1. Notice your trigger moments

For a day or two, notice when you start scrolling. Common triggers:

  • waiting (for a file, a call, the kettle)
  • the moment you get stuck on a task
  • right after waking up or right before bed
  • feeling anxious or bored

You can't fix a habit you can't see. Most people find two or three triggers cause most of their scrolling.

2. Replace, don't just remove

If you remove scrolling from a trigger moment, something else will fill it. Decide in advance what that is: a glass of water, a stretch, one page of a book, tidying your desk. Boring is fine. It just needs to be easier than unlocking your phone.

3. Add friction

Tiny obstacles work surprisingly well:

  • Log out of the apps you scroll most.
  • Move them off your home screen, or into a folder on the last page.
  • Block the specific feed pages on your computer (for example, just the Reels or Shorts pages, not the whole site).

4. Turn off non-human notifications

Keep notifications from actual people (messages, calls). Turn off everything else: likes, "suggested for you", trending, breaking-news pushes. Every notification is an invitation to start scrolling.

5. Keep your phone out of reach during focused work

Not on silent next to your keyboard, but in a bag or another room. Studies on phone proximity suggest that simply having a phone nearby can pull at your attention even when you don't touch it.

6. Use time limits as speed bumps

Your phone's built-in tools (Screen Time on iPhone, Digital Wellbeing on Android) can limit specific apps. You can override them, but the pause they create is often enough to make you ask, "do I actually want this?"

The best time limit is the one that interrupts you. If you ignore it every day, lower the limit or put the app further away.

7. Schedule the scroll

Banning something outright often backfires. Instead, pick a time: "I scroll on my lunch break" or "after 7pm". When the urge hits at 11am, the answer isn't no, it's later. That's much easier to accept.

8. Be kind about the slip

You'll lose half an hour to a feed sometimes. Instead of "well, the day's ruined", do the smallest next thing: set a 10-minute timer and do one task. Momentum comes back faster than you think.

The bottom line

Doomscrolling isn't about weak willpower; the feed is doing exactly what it was built to do. Know your triggers, add a little friction, keep your phone out of reach while you focus, and give yourself a better default. Small changes, repeated, beat big resolutions every time.
